    1. Sociolinguistic Overview: A Romano-Germanic Imperial Tongue

    Formed on 31 August 989 from the hybridization of Lothringian (a bureaucratic Franconian culture speaking High German) and Catalan (a courtly, maritime Occitano-Romance culture of the Spanish March / Hispania Tarraconensis), Tarraconian (Tarracono-Lothringian, ID 316) represents a historical “reverse Frankish” linguistic genesis.

    Rather than abandoning their Germanic tongue for Romance, the Lothringian imperial elite of House Chatenois-Metz retained 11th/12th-century High German (language_high_german) and Franconian personal names (name_list_franconian), while absorbing Catalan courtly culture (ethos_courtly), Mediterranean maritime mercantilism, Iberian architecture, and an Imperial Magistracy system of governance.

    The result is a language structurally parallel to Middle English (Germanic grammar + Anglo-Norman vocabulary), but with roles and geographies reversed:

    • Core Grammar, Morphology & Phonology: Early Middle High German (Moselle/Rhine Franconian dialect of Metz, Trier, and Mainz).
    • Administrative, Courtly & Nautical Lexicon: Old Catalan, Occitan, and Medieval Hispano-Latin, supplemented after the Imperial Restoration by Byzantine Greek administrative borrowings.
    • Chancellery Orthography: High German phonemes written through the scribal conventions of Catalan-Aragonese and Imperial chancelleries.

    2. Orthography & IPA Pronunciation Guide

    Tarraconian scribes write their Moselle-Franconian High German using a blend of Middle High German and Old Catalan spelling conventions. Circumflexes (â, ê, î, ô, û / Â, Ê, Î, Ô, Û) systematically indicate long vowels. Capitalization does not alter vowel quantity: capital letters systematically retain their circumflexes (e.g. Ôsterrîch, Îslant, Âchen, Ûtrecht, Êvore).

    3. Core Phonological & Phonotactic Rules

    Rule 1 Rhine-Moselle Franconian Consonant Profile

    Because Tarraconian descends from Lothringian (West Central German), post-vocalic *k shifted to -ch (Rîch) and *t shifted to z- / tz (Zips, Mênz). However, unlike Upper German (Bavarian/Alemannic), Tarraconian preserves initial unshifted p- (Palz, Port), unshifted d- (Dat, Dorf, Nider-), and preserves Middle High German sw-, sl-, sm- (Swâben, Slêswîch, Smalkalden) without New High German schw-/schl-/schm- spellings.

    Rule 2 Universal Auslautverhärtung (Final Devoicing)

    Middle High German and Old Catalan systematically devoice word-final obstruents across all etymological strata:

    • Word-final -g and -k merge into -ch ([x ~ ç]): Burg → Burch, Berg → Berch, Mark → March, Baalbek → Bâlbech.
    • Word-final -d and -b devoice to -t and -p: Land → Lant (Dütschlant, Engellant), Baghdad → Bagdât, Ḥalab → Halep.
    • Word-final affricate *-dsch is strictly illicit; it devoices to -tsch: Najd → Netsch, Aflaj → Falâtsch.
    • Foreign word-final -ov / -ev / -av vocalizes to the MHG diphthongal suffix -ouwe ([ɔʊ.wə]): Opava → Troppouwe, Moskva → Moskouwe, Rostov → Rostouwe.
    Rule 3 Dual-Substrate Apocope & Vowel Reduction

    Catalan apocope drops unstressed final -o, -e after liquids and nasals (Tarracona → Tarracôn, Tudela → Tudêl), while High German reduces unstressed final vowels to schwa -e [ə] (Praga → Prâge, Lucca → Lûke, Huesca → Oske). Greek -ion yields -jen (Ikônjen), while -ea / -eia yields -êe (Kaisarêe, Nîkêe).

    Rule 4 Illicit Cluster Repair & Slavic -sk Adaptation

    Foreign onset clusters that violate sonority sequencing insert a short vowel epenthesis or prosthesis (Wschowa → Wischouwe, Trnovo → Ternouwe, Mtskheta → Metzchête, Pset → Pesêt). Bare word-final Slavic -sk is resolved to Franconian -tzich [tsɪç] (Połock → Polôtzich, Płock → Plôtzich, Słupsk → Slûptzich, Smoleńsk → Smolêntzich) or labial -bsch (Vitebsk → Wîtebsch).

    Rule 5 Word Stress & Bimoraic Weight Condition

    Stressed syllables in Tarraconian must be bimoraic (σstressed ≥ μμ): open stressed syllables require a long circumflex vowel or diphthong (Prâge [ˈpraː.ɣə], Spîre [ˈʃpiː.rə]), while short vowels must be closed by a coda consonant (March [ˈmarx], Batsch [ˈbatʃ]). Heavy syllables attract primary stress (Tarracôn, Adrianôpel, Lantschân).

    • Alpine-Tarraconian (Alpîn-Tarraconisch, 1042 AD): Spoken in Cisalpine Italy; fronts long û → [yː], nasalizes vowels before coda -n (Meilân [mɛɪ.ˈlaːŋ]), and softens affricates.
    • Croato-Tarraconian (Kroatisch-Tarraconisch, 1057 AD): Spoken in Dalmatia and Dinaric marches; distinguishes alveolo-palatal [tɕ] from postalveolar [tʃ] and retains trilled syllabic [r̩].
    • Ungwârer (Ungwârerisch / Ungwâri, 1094 AD): Germano-Magyar mixed daughter language (Central Germanic heritage + Magyar tongue) formed when Tarraconian castle-wardens intermarried with Hungarian horse-archers in Upper Hungary (Ungwâr / Užhorod).

    Phonetic transliterations strictly reflect the phonological systems active in the 12th century (ca. 1154 AD), rather than modern national languages (Modern Standard Mandarin / Pinyin, Modern Tokyo Japanese, Modern Seoul Korean):

    • Late Middle Chinese (Song/Jin): Full retention of entering tone stops (-p, -t, -k) and 36 initials; unmerged retroflex vs dental sibilants.

    1. The Doctrine of Imperial Title Symbols (Signa Imperii)

    Unlike feudal Western Europe—where personal triangular heater shields and dynastic quarterings emerged from multinational Crusades and the hereditary patrimonialization of fiefs—the restored Roman Empire (1059–1194) is an Administrative Realm governing 2,644 counties across 47 empires and 213 kingdoms.

    Because provincial governorships (Regenate, Gubernate, Magistrate) are appointed administrative offices (appointment_succession) rather than private hereditary property, the symbol belongs to the office and jurisdiction (Amtszeichen), not the temporary governor's lineage.

    2. The Five Physical Media by De Jure Rank

    • Imperial Vexillum (Empire): Fringed silk square standard mounted on a golden crossbar with cords, tassels, and imperial finials, derived from the Late Roman vexillum and the 69 in-save warbanners.
    • Three-Tailed Gonfanon (Kingdom / Regenat): Carolingian and Catalan investiture warbanner (Amtsvanen) with three swallow-tailed streamers.
    • Notitia Command Shield (Duchy / Gubernat): Oval or round Late-Roman command shield with metallic umbo (boss) and radiating provincial border rim, directly evoking the Notitia Dignitatum.
    • Circular Chancellery Bulla (County / Magistrat): Red wax or cast lead bulla with an outer Latin/Tarraconian epigraphic ring (+ SIGILLVM MAGISTRATVS ...) enclosing the municipal/civic canting charge.
    • Sinosphere Vermilion Chop (Procedure 1 Counties): Square carved jade/ivory seal impression (Guóxī) stamped in vermilion cinnabar ink, bearing classical Hanzi logographs and corner meander ornaments.
    • Pointed Mandorla / Vesica Piscis (Ecclesiastical): Archiepiscopal and patriarchal ecclesiastical seal under the Caesaropapist authority of the Pope-Emperor.
